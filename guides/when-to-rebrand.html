A rebrand can unlock growth, or it can throw away years of recognition overnight. The difference is usually whether there’s a real business reason behind it. Here’s how to tell.

Refresh, rebrand or rename?

ChangeWhat it involvesRisk
RefreshModernising logo, colours, type and templates while keeping the core recognisable.Low
RebrandNew positioning and identity; often new messaging and experience.Medium–high
RenameA new name, usually with a new identity.High

Most brands that think they need a rebrand actually need a refresh plus better consistency.

Seven good reasons to rebrand

  1. Your strategy changed. New audience, new category or a new business model that the old brand can’t carry.
  2. A merger or acquisition. Two brands must become one, or a clear architecture must be defined.
  3. Legal problems. A trademark conflict or inability to protect your name.
  4. Reputation damage. When a name is inseparable from a crisis — though fixing the underlying problem comes first.
  5. You’ve outgrown it. A local or founder-era identity that limits expansion into new markets or premium positions.
  6. You’re indistinguishable. Customers confuse you with competitors, and research shows it.
  7. The identity doesn’t work. It fails on mobile, at small sizes, in accessibility checks or across products.

Five bad reasons

  1. The team is bored of it. Customers see your brand far less than you do.
  2. A new leader wants to make a mark.
  3. A competitor just rebranded.
  4. To cover a product problem.
  5. Because a trend looks fresh this year.

Before you decide: measure what you’d lose

Audit your current brand equity: recognition of your name, colours and logo, branded search volume, customer descriptions of you, and assets customers clearly associate with you. Keep what works. Many successful rebrands evolve a recognisable element — a colour, a shape, a word — rather than replacing everything. The brand audit is a quick first pass.

A rebrand rollout plan

  1. Strategy. Re-confirm positioning, audience and personality. Write the case for change in one page.
  2. Design. Develop the identity with real applications, not just a logo on a white background.
  3. Legal. Clear and file trademarks; secure domains and handles.
  4. Inventory. List every touchpoint — website, apps, social, email, documents, packaging, signage, vehicles, uniforms, partner listings.
  5. Prioritise. Switch digital touchpoints on launch day; schedule physical ones by cost and visibility.
  6. Internal launch. Brief employees first with the story, the guide and templates.
  7. External launch. Tell customers why it changed and what it means for them — briefly.
  8. Redirects and SEO. If the name or domain changes, set up 301 redirects, update business listings and tell search engines about the move.
  9. Measure. Track recognition, branded search and customer feedback for six to twelve months.
Budget reality: rollout often costs more than design. Signage, packaging, vehicles and printed materials can dwarf the identity fee — plan both.
