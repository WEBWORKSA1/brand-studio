A personal brand is simply what people say about you and your work when you’re not in the room — and what they find when they search your name. You already have one. The question is whether it’s deliberate.

1. Pick a lane

Choose the intersection you want to be known for: a skill, an audience and a point of view. “Brand designer” is a job title. “Brand designer who helps climate startups look as credible as incumbents” is a reputation. Narrow is memorable; you can widen later.

2. Define your promise and proof

Write one sentence describing the result people get from working with you, then list proof: projects, numbers, testimonials, credentials. If the proof is thin, your first job is to create it — side projects, case studies, pro-bono work for a cause you care about.

3. Find your voice

Choose three words that describe how you want to come across — for example practical, generous, candid. The archetype quiz is a quick way to find a starting point. Then write like you speak: short sentences, real examples, no borrowed jargon.

4. Craft your story

People remember stories, not CVs. Prepare three versions: a one-line introduction, a 30-second answer to “what do you do?” and a short origin story explaining why you do this work.

5. Build a simple visual identity

  • A consistent, recent, well-lit headshot across platforms.
  • One or two signature colours and a font pair — the palette builder and font pairing tool are free.
  • A clean wordmark of your name for your website, proposals and slides.

6. Own your home base

Social platforms change; your own website and email list don’t. Register your name as a domain if you can, publish a simple site with your promise, proof, work samples and a contact path, and invite people to subscribe.

7. Choose one or two platforms and show up

Go where your audience already pays attention. Publish consistently around two or three themes. A useful rhythm: share what you’re learning, what you’ve made, and what you think about your field. Engage with others in your niche — comments and collaborations often grow a reputation faster than posts.

8. Stay consistent and measure

Use the same name, photo, bio and colours everywhere. Every quarter, search your name in a private browser window and review what comes up. Track inbound opportunities, not just follower counts.

Privacy reminder: decide what you won’t share publicly — family, location, finances — before you start. A strong personal brand doesn’t require an open diary.
