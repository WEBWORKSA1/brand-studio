Most founders start branding at the logo. Studios start somewhere else entirely: with the question of who this is for and why it should exist. The visual work comes later, and it’s faster and better because the strategy did the hard thinking first.

This playbook condenses a typical studio process into ten steps. Small brands can move through it in a focused week; larger organisations spread it over months. Either way, the order matters.

1. Define the job your brand does

Write one sentence describing the change a customer experiences because you exist. Not what you sell — what changes. “We roast coffee” is a product. “We make the first ten minutes of your day feel like a ritual, not a chore” is a job.

This sentence becomes the filter for every later decision. If a name, colour or tagline doesn’t support it, it doesn’t make the cut.

2. Know your audience better than they expect

Describe your primary customer in a paragraph: what they’re trying to achieve, what frustrates them about current options, where they spend attention and what they’d be embarrassed to be seen using. Then go and test it. Five short conversations with real prospects will correct more assumptions than a week of desk research.

  • Ask what they use today and what they wish it did.
  • Ask how they’d describe the ideal solution to a friend — note the exact words.
  • Ask what would make them switch, and what would stop them.

The words customers use are often better copy than anything you’ll write.

3. Map the competition and find the gap

List your top five competitors, including the “do nothing” option. For each, note their promise, price position, personality and visual cues. Plot them on two axes that matter to your customer — for example premium ↔ affordable and expert ↔ friendly. Empty quadrants are opportunities; crowded ones are where you’ll be invisible.

Different vs distinctive: being meaningfully different (a real advantage) wins the choice. Being distinctive (instantly recognisable) wins the memory. Strong brands work on both.

4. Write your positioning statement

Use this classic structure and fill it in honestly:

For [audience] who [need], [Brand] is the [category] that [key benefit]. Unlike [alternative], we [difference], because [reason to believe].

It’s an internal tool, not a tagline. If you can’t complete the “because” clause with proof, your positioning isn’t yet credible — fix the product or the proof before you fix the copy. A copy-ready version is on our templates page.

5. Choose a personality

People relate to brands the way they relate to people. Decide who yours is at a dinner party: the expert everyone asks, the rebel who says what others won’t, the host who makes everyone comfortable. Brand archetypes are a helpful shortcut here — our two-minute quiz gives you a primary and secondary archetype plus voice words.

Turn the result into a three-row voice chart: we are confident, not arrogant; warm, not gushing; clear, not simplistic.

6. Name it (or stress-test the name you have)

A good name is easy to say, spell and remember, works in your key markets, and can be legally protected. Generate widely — fifty to a hundred candidates — then filter hard. Our naming guide covers the full method, and the name generator gives you a fast first batch.

Before you fall in love, run a knock-out trademark search and check domains with the trademark launcher. Renaming after launch costs far more than renaming before.

7. Build the visual identity system

An identity is a system, not a logo. At minimum you need:

ElementWhat “done” looks like
LogoPrimary lockup, a compact version and a square icon; full colour, one colour and reversed.
ColourFive roles — primary, secondary, accent, light, dark — with HEX, RGB and CMYK. Text pairs pass contrast checks.
TypographyOne headline face, one body face, with sizes and weights defined.
ImageryA short description of photo style, illustration style or both.
Graphic devicesOptional shapes, patterns or icon style that make layouts recognisable.

Start with the palette builder and font pairing tool, then set your name in the wordmark maker. Test everything small: if your logo fails at 16 pixels, simplify.

8. Write the words

Brands are read more than they’re seen. Draft these core assets before you build a website:

  • Tagline — a short promise you can keep. Try the slogan generator for starting points.
  • Homepage hero — what you do, for whom, in under ten words, plus one action.
  • Boilerplates — 25-, 50- and 100-word descriptions for profiles and press.
  • Key messages — three proof-backed reasons to choose you.

9. Document it in a brand guide

Consistency is what turns a set of nice assets into a brand. Write it down. A one-page guide with your mission, audience, personality, colours, fonts, logo rules and a short do/don’t list is enough for most small teams. The brand guide builder creates one and exports a PDF.

Name an owner for the brand and send the guide to every freelancer, printer and new hire. Review it quarterly.

10. Launch, then measure

Roll the brand out across every touchpoint at once if you can — website, social profiles, email signatures, invoices, packaging and signage. A half-launched brand looks like two companies.

Then measure what brands actually move: branded search volume, direct traffic, referral rates, the share of customers who mention you by name, and the price premium you can hold. Run the brand audit again every quarter and fix the lowest-scoring pillar.

Common mistakes to avoid

  1. Designing before deciding. A logo can’t fix unclear positioning.
  2. Copying the category leader. You’ll look like a cheaper version of them.
  3. Too many colours and fonts. Restraint reads as confidence.
  4. Skipping legal checks. A cease-and-desist after launch is the most expensive branding lesson.
  5. Changing too often. Consistency compounds; most brands get bored of themselves long before customers do.

DIY or hire?

DIY is fine for validation and early traction. Hire professionals when the stakes rise: you’re raising money, entering retail, rebranding an established business or competing in a crowded premium category. Read how to choose a branding agency and what branding costs before you brief anyone.
