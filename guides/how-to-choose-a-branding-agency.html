Hiring a branding partner is one of the few marketing decisions you’ll live with for a decade. The right partner clarifies your strategy and gives you assets that compound. The wrong one gives you a pretty logo and an invoice. Here’s how to choose well.

Freelancer, studio or agency?

OptionBest forTrade-offs
Senior freelancerStartups and small businesses needing identity and basic guidelines.Limited capacity; strategy depth varies.
Boutique studioGrowing companies wanting strategy plus craft.Smaller teams; may partner for web or naming.
Full-service agencyComplex rebrands, multiple stakeholders, large rollouts.Higher cost; you may see senior staff less after the pitch.
SpecialistNaming, packaging, motion or specific industries.Narrow scope; coordinate with other partners.

Write a brief first

A good brief halves the time to a good proposal. Include your background, objectives, audience, competitors, scope and deliverables, budget range, timeline, decision-makers and how you’ll judge success. Our creative brief template gives you the structure. Always share a budget range — it lets partners propose the right scope instead of guessing.

Build a shortlist of three to five

  • Look for work in your category or a similar challenge (for example, “premium repositioning”).
  • Read case studies, not just portfolio images. Do they explain the problem and the result?
  • Check that their typical project size matches your budget.
  • Ask peers whose brands you admire who they used.

Twelve questions to ask

  1. What’s your process, step by step, and where do we make decisions?
  2. Who exactly will work on our project, and how senior are they?
  3. How do you research our audience and competitors?
  4. Can you walk us through a project with a similar challenge?
  5. How many concepts do you present, and how many revision rounds are included?
  6. What deliverables are included — files, formats, guidelines, applications?
  7. How do you handle trademark considerations for names and logos?
  8. Who owns the work and when does copyright transfer?
  9. How do you price — fixed fee, phases or hourly — and what triggers extra cost?
  10. What do you need from us, and how much of our time?
  11. How do you measure whether the work succeeded?
  12. Can we speak to two recent clients?

Red flags

  • Spec work offered for free to win the pitch — it skips discovery and undervalues the craft.
  • No questions about your business before quoting.
  • Vague deliverables (“logo and branding”).
  • No written ownership transfer.
  • Portfolio pieces that look derivative of famous brands.
  • A senior team at the pitch that vanishes once you sign.

Compare proposals fairly

Line proposals up against the same criteria: understanding of your problem, process, team, deliverables, timeline, price and chemistry. The cheapest proposal is often missing discovery or rollout; the most expensive may include things you don’t need yet. Ask both to adjust scope to your priorities.

Pricing models: fixed fees with phase payments (e.g. 40/30/30) suit defined identity projects. Hourly or retainer pricing suits ongoing or uncertain work. Either way, agree in writing what happens when scope changes.

Set the project up to succeed

  • Name one decision-maker and keep the approval group small.
  • Give feedback against the brief, not personal taste.
  • Share real constraints early — budget, deadlines, legal issues.
  • Plan rollout budget alongside design budget.
