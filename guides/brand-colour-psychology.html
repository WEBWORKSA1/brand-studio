Colour is usually the first thing people notice about a brand and one of the fastest routes to recognition. But much of what’s written about colour psychology — “blue means trust, red means hunger” — is oversimplified. Here’s what holds up, what doesn’t, and how to choose colours that work.

What the research actually supports

  • Fit matters more than any single colour. Studies of colour and branding consistently find that people respond to whether a colour feels appropriate for the brand and product, rather than to fixed meanings of individual colours.
  • Colour aids recognition. Consistent use of a distinctive colour helps people pick a brand out quickly — think of how you find a familiar product on a crowded shelf.
  • Associations are cultural and learned. White is linked with purity in many Western contexts and with mourning in parts of East Asia. Red signals luck and celebration in China, danger or urgency in many other contexts.
  • Context changes everything. The same green reads as “eco” on packaging and as “success” on a dashboard.
Treat colour meanings as tendencies, not rules. Your category, audience, culture and competitors matter more than a generic colour chart.

A practical method for choosing brand colours

1. Start from personality

List your three voice words and archetype (the archetype quiz gives you a starting direction). Ask which hues, saturation and brightness feel like those words. Brightness and saturation often matter as much as hue: a muted navy and an electric blue say very different things.

2. Map competitors

Collect your top competitors’ primary colours. If everyone in your category is blue, a well-chosen alternative may be your biggest distinctiveness win.

3. Pick one colour to own

Choose a single primary you’ll use relentlessly. Consistency over years, not novelty, is what makes a colour “yours”.

4. Build the system around it

Add a secondary, an accent, a light background and a dark text colour. The palette builder does this using colour harmony rules and lets you lock and edit swatches.

5. Check accessibility

Under WCAG 2.2 level AA, normal text needs a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 against its background and large text at least 3:1. Many bright brand colours fail as text on white — use them for shapes and buttons with dark text instead.

6. Test in context

Apply the palette to a homepage, a social post, a product and a printed card. Colours that look harmonious as swatches can feel very different at scale.

Screen vs print

Define each colour in HEX and RGB for screens, CMYK for four-colour print and, if you print large quantities or need exact matching, a Pantone spot colour. Bright screen colours may not reproduce in CMYK, so check a printed proof before committing.

Mistakes to avoid

  1. Choosing a colour because you personally like it.
  2. Using too many equally strong colours.
  3. Ignoring contrast and accessibility.
  4. Copying the category leader’s colour.
  5. Changing colours every year — distinctiveness needs time.
