Your name is the one brand asset you’ll use in every conversation, on every invoice and in every search. It’s also the hardest to change later. A good naming process doesn’t rely on inspiration; it generates widely, filters ruthlessly and checks legally before anyone gets attached.

Start with a naming brief

Before generating a single name, answer five questions in writing:

  1. What should the name signal? Pick two or three qualities, such as trustworthy and modern, or playful and premium.
  2. Who must understand it? Which countries and languages matter in the next five years?
  3. What will it name? A company, a product, or a family of products? Will it need to stretch to new categories?
  4. What are competitors called? List them so you can avoid sounding like them.
  5. What are the must-avoids? Words with unfortunate meanings, hard-to-spell sounds, or anything too close to an existing mark.

Understand the main name types

DescriptiveSays what you do (“City Plumbing”).Instantly clear.Hard to trademark; easy to confuse with rivals.
SuggestiveHints at a benefit (“Brightwater”).Meaningful and protectable.Popular words are crowded.
CompoundTwo words fused (“Northwind”).Distinct, often available.Can feel generic if both halves are common.
InventedCoined word (e.g. “Quenzo”).Strongest legal protection, global.Needs marketing to give it meaning.
MetaphorReal word, borrowed meaning (“Atlas”).Emotional, memorable.Many companies may share it.

AcronymInitials.Short.Forgettable unless you already have scale.

Trademark law generally favours names further from description. Invented and arbitrary names are typically the easiest to protect; purely descriptive names are often refused unless they’ve acquired distinctiveness through use.

Generate at volume

Aim for 100 candidates before you judge any of them. Use several methods:

  • Word banks. List words around your benefit, your process, the feeling you create, and imagery from nature, motion, light or myth.
  • Construction patterns. Combine, blend, add prefixes and suffixes, respell. Our name generator runs eight of these patterns instantly.
  • Other languages. Latin, Greek and other languages offer roots — but check the meaning with native speakers.
  • Shorten and twist. Truncate long words; swap a letter; join a verb and a noun.

Don’t edit while generating. The weird options often lead to the good ones.

Filter like a professional

Cut your list to ten using quick tests, then to three using deeper ones.

Quick tests

  • Phone test: say it once. Can the listener spell it?
  • Length: under three syllables is easier; under ten letters is better for domains and logos.
  • Fit: does it match two of your brief’s qualities?
  • Crowd: search it with your category. Are there similar names nearby?

Deeper tests

  • Linguistic screening: check meanings and pronunciation in every key market.
  • Visual test: set it in a few fonts. Some names look great; others look clumsy.
  • Story test: can you explain the name in one sentence that reinforces your positioning?
  • Stretch test: would it still work if you added a new product line?

Run the legal and availability checks

This is the step founders skip and regret. For each finalist:

  1. Search your main trademark office and WIPO’s Global Brand Database for identical and similar marks in the classes you’ll trade in. Our trademark launcher opens all the major offices at once.
  2. Check company registers in your country for identical business names.
  3. Check domains. You don’t need the exact .com, but you need a domain people will type correctly — a modifier like “get”, “try” or “hq”, or a strong country or industry extension, can work.
  4. Check social handles on the platforms that matter to you.

Then pay a trademark professional for a clearance opinion on your top choice before you print anything. The fee is small compared with a rebrand. Read How to trademark a brand name for the filing steps.

Test with real people

Show your top three names to people in your audience — without explaining them — and ask what kind of company each sounds like, what it might cost and whether they’d remember it tomorrow. You’re not looking for “I like it”; you’re looking for whether the name signals what your brief asked for.

Every name feels strange at first. Familiarity grows quickly once a name is attached to a real product and consistent design. Judge against the brief, not against your gut on day one.

Decide and commit

Pick the name that best meets the brief and clears legally, secure the domain and handles the same day, and file your trademark application as soon as you’re confident. Then move straight to identity: build a palette, choose fonts and set your wordmark.
